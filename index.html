How Sensor Delay Affects
Robot Stopping Distance


San Bernardino Science & Engineering Fair

Presenter: Tianrui Li

Abstract

Purpose: This study investigates the critical safety margins in autonomous systems by analyzing how sensor processing delay, initial motor power, and floor surface friction affect a robot's stopping distance.

Methodology: A rigorous factorial design consisting of 120 independent trials was conducted. The robot was tested across 5 delay levels (10-500ms), 4 power levels (25-100%), and 2 distinct surface materials.

Key Findings: Statistical analysis revealed a strong negative correlation (r = -0.810) between sensor delay and stopping distance. The data strongly suggests that computational latency is a more dominant risk factor in collisions than initial velocity.

1. Introduction

In real-world applications, autonomous systems—such as self-driving cars and warehouse AGVs—experience inherent processing latency between detecting an obstacle and executing a full stop. This delay neutralizes safety algorithms and causes physical collisions.

2. Research Question

How do programmed sensor delay, initial motor power, and kinetic friction from the floor surface mathematically interact to determine the final distance between a moving robot and a wall?

3. Hypotheses

  • Delay: Increasing sensor delay will drastically decrease the final distance, as momentum carries the chassis forward unbraked.
  • Power: Higher power (speed) will result in a shorter stopping distance due to increased inertia.
  • Surface: High-friction surfaces (Yoga Mat) will decelerate the robot faster, leaving a safer distance.

Variables & Significance

Significance: Modeling these factors on a small-scale robot provides actionable insights into how commercial autonomous vehicles should prioritize hardware upgrades (e.g., faster CPU vs. better brakes).

Experimental Variables:

  • Independent Variables:
    1. Sensor delay (10, 50, 100, 200, 500 ms)
    2. Initial motor power (25%, 50%, 75%, 100%)
    3. Floor surface (Low friction: Wooden floor vs. High friction: Yoga mat).
  • Dependent Variable: Final stopping distance measured from the wall (cm).
  • Controlled Variables: Robot chassis, battery charge level, starting position, and fixed obstacle.

Materials & Design

Hardware Setup:

  • Lafvitech 2WD Programmable Mobile Robot.
  • HC-SR04 Ultrasonic Obstacle Sensor for distance detection.
  • Arduino UNO (C++) for logic execution and delay simulation.

Factorial Design:

To ensure data reliability and account for outliers, the experiment utilized a full factorial design. Every combination of delay, power, and surface was tested with 3 repetitions, yielding exactly 120 unique data points.

Procedure & Data Collection

Execution Protocol:

  1. Position the robot at the designated starting line on the selected surface.
  2. Upload the specific delay and power parameters via Arduino IDE.
  3. Activate the robot and allow the ultrasonic sensor to detect the wall and trigger the braking mechanism.
  4. Measure the final resting distance using a fixed measuring tape.

Safety & Extreme Condition:

Low voltage was maintained to prevent hardware damage. If the robot failed to stop and touched the wall, a distance of 0 cm (Collision) was formally recorded.

Data Analysis & Correlation

Methodology: Data was aggregated using Python (Pandas). Scikit-learn was utilized to extract Pearson correlation coefficients and build predictive models.

Correlation Findings:

The generated Heatmap revealed a coefficient of r = -0.810 between Sensor Delay and Distance to wall.

This confirms a strong negative correlation, statistically validating that as latency increases, safety margin decreases rapidly.

Regression Model & Interpretation

A Multiple Linear Regression model was deployed, explaining 71% of the total variance (R² = 0.710).

Y = 20.095 - 0.035(Delay) - 0.960(Power) + 3.443(Surface)

Statistical Significance (p-values):

  • Delay & Surface (p < 0.05): Highly significant. The Yoga mat added ~3.44 cm of safety distance due to friction.
  • Motor Power (p = 0.48): Exceeded 0.05, indicating it is not statistically significant in this linear model, contrary to initial assumptions.

Extreme Condition: Collision

The most critical observation occurred at the extreme latency setting.

At a programmed delay of 500 ms, the robot consistently recorded distances of 0 cm across almost all power levels.

This phenomenon demonstrates that a sensor's accuracy becomes irrelevant if the system's processing time allows physical momentum to overtake the braking zone. The robot essentially crashed before the Arduino executed the stop command.

Discussion & Conclusion

Hypothesis Evaluation: The hypotheses were partially supported. Delay and surface friction behaved as expected, but motor power did not linearly impact the final distance as significantly as predicted.

Core Conclusion:

In autonomous engineering, computational speed is paramount. A delay of merely half a second (500ms) is enough to neutralize a vehicle's collision avoidance system entirely. Furthermore, environmental factors like road friction (simulated by the Yoga mat) act as critical passive safety buffers that can compensate for minor system latencies.

Limitations & Future Research

Experimental Limitations:

  • Motor power percentages (25-100%) were an approximation of output, not a direct measurement of velocity (m/s).
  • The "0 cm" result grouped minor bumper touches and severe hard crashes into the same numerical value.

Future Engineering Applications:

  • Develop an adaptive braking algorithm that actively calculates surface friction and adjusts stopping force accordingly.
  • Implement a machine-learning logistic regression model to predict the *probability* of a collision rather than just the distance.
